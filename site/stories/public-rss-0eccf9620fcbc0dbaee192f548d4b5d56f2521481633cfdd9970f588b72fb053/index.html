    多梅尼卡利解释F1为何提前回归V8引擎

    F1首席执行官斯特凡诺·多梅尼卡利解释了为何提前推进V8引擎回归，称当初影响2026规则制定的“生存”之争已不再需要。F1与FIA周五宣布一致同意V8新规于2030年启用，比原定2031年提前一年。

    F1首席执行官斯特凡诺·多梅尼卡利解释了为何V8引擎回归被提前推进，称当初影响2026规则制定的“生存”之争已不再需要。

    当前的50/50涡轮混合动力单元尚未完成首个赛季的比赛，但F1与FIA于周五宣布，各方一致同意新的V8规则于2030年生效。

    这一戏剧性的方向转变，尽管多梅尼卡利此前曾强力捍卫现行规则——这些规则因让赛车能量匮乏而在车迷中引发分歧。

    这也比最初厂商与赛事利益相关方商定50/50规则时设定的2031年时间表提前了一年。

    周五在拉斯维加斯举行的 Axios 与 The Race 联合活动“超越赛道：创新与领导力投资”上，多梅尼卡利向 The Race 联合创始人达伦·考克斯表示，他阐述了新引擎为何合理。

    多梅尼卡利解释说，2026 年的规则实际上并非 F1 主动推动的事项。

    相反，它们是必然之举，由两个因素共同触发——当时正值新冠疫情引发全球金融不确定性的时期，大奖赛管理层正在规划未来规则。

    首先，当时制造商们寻求全面电动化的未来，多梅尼卡利表示，F1 反对完全依赖电池的立场意味着必须达成一种 50/50 的折中方案。

    其次，在新冠疫情造成的经济冲击下，如果失去制造商，F1将面临重大风险，因此它并不处于强势地位，无法抵制制造商的要求。

    正如多梅尼卡利所说：“有必要做我们现在正在做的事情，否则我们就不会有赛车了。就这么简单。

    “所以这就是为什么管理起来要复杂得多。但这也正是美妙之处，因为这给了我们重新创造、尝试书写新故事（并）创造新关注点的可能性。”

    在2020年底至2021年初有一段时间，红牛尚未完全承诺其引擎计划，雷诺在讨论该怎么做，奥迪尚未签约，在最坏的情况下，法拉利和梅赛德斯可能不得不被说服为所有车队供应引擎。

    这可能是他们此前不愿做的事情，而那样做会让F1陷入危机局面。

    这就是为什么对F1和FIA来说，制定规则让所有制造商满意、尤其是说服奥迪加入，被证明如此关键。

    多梅尼卡利解释说，如今世界已大不相同，转向更便宜、更轻、更受欢迎的V8引擎是显而易见的选择——因为F1不再需要如此迁就汽车制造商的需求。

    当被问及F1为何如此迅速地偏离现行规则时，多梅尼卡利表示：“很简单。因为我们希望与未来保持相关性。”

    “我确实相信，我们之所以做出改变，是因为需要保持吸引力并与技术保持相关性。”

    “当时，在与国际汽联、制造商或汽车行业的规则决策中，大家都想走电动化，而我们不想走电动化，就这样。”

    “那么平衡点是什么？走向混合动力。所以是50比50，不管具体比例如何。我们知道那是当时生存下去的必要条件。”

    “而且，我们不是愚蠢的人。因此我们与很多人进行了讨论：车手、车队、制造商。这种演变是自然而然的。”

    尽管F1过去曾因陷入无休止的规则变更循环而受到批评——这些规则似乎从未获得足够时间稳固下来——但多梅尼卡利认为，原地踏步同样糟糕。

    “我回到一点：不改变的运动正在失去机会，”他说。

    当被问及反复修订规则可能阻碍F1的推广时，多梅尼卡利表示：“恰恰相反。阻碍在于一项不理解变革需求的运动。

    “我的意思是，你需要有吸引力，并理解大众的走向。”

    英文原文

    Formula 1 CEO Stefano Domenicali has explained why a return to V8 engines has been fast-tracked, with a fight to “survive” that influenced the 2026 rules no longer needed.

    The current 50/50 turbo hybrid power units have yet to complete their first season of racing, but F1 and the FIA announced on Friday that there was unanimous agreement for new V8 regulations to come into play for 2030.

    The dramatic change of direction comes despite Domenicali’s robust defence of the current regulations that have divided opinions among fans because of the way they have left cars so energy-starved.

    It is also one year earlier than the 2031 timeframe that was laid down when the 50/50 rules were originally agreed between manufacturers and championship stakeholders.

    Speaking to The Race co-founder Darren Cox at Axios and The Race’s Beyond the Race: Investments in Innovation and Leadership event in Las Vegas on Friday, Domenicali offered insight into why new engines make sense.

    Domenicali explained that the 2026 regulations were not actually something that F1 had actively been pushing for.

    Instead, they were a necessity triggered by two factors coming together as grand prix chiefs planned future rules amid the times of global financial uncertainty triggered by the Covid pandemic.

    First, with manufacturers looking back then for an all-electric future, Domenicali says a pushback from F1 against going full battery meant a compromise 50/50 element had to be settled upon.

    And secondly, amid the economic fallout of the Covid pandemic, F1 faced major risks if it lost manufacturers, so it was not in a position of strength to resist what they wanted.

    As Domenicali said: “There was the need to do what we are doing now, because otherwise we would not have had the cars. Simple as that.

    “So that’s why it’s a much more complicated exercise to manage. But that’s also the beauty, because this gives us the possibility of reinventing, trying to create a new story, [and] create new attention.”

    There was a period in late 2020/early 2021, with Red Bull not yet fully committed to its engine plans, Renault debating what to do, and Audi not having signed up, that in a worst-case scenario Ferrari and Mercedes could have needed to be leaned on to supply all teams.

    This is something they may not have been willing to do, which would have put F1 in a crisis situation.

    This is why it proved so critical for F1 and the FIA to create rules that kept all manufacturers happy and especially convinced Audi to join.

    Domenicali explained that, with the world in a different place now, moving to cheaper, lighter and more popular V8s was a no-brainer – as F1 did not need to kowtow to the needs of car makers so much.

    Asked about why F1 was so quickly moving away from the current rules, Domenicali said: “Very simple. Because we want to be relevant for the future.

    “I do believe that the reason why we are changing is because there is the need to be attractive and relevant to the technology.

    “At that moment there was the decision on the regulations with the FIA, the manufacturers, or the automotive industry, wanted to be electric, and we didn’t want to go electric, full stop.

    “So what is the balance? Going to be hybrid. So 50-50, whatever it is. And we knew that was a need to survive in that moment.

    “And then, we are not stupid people. And therefore we were having discussions with a lot of people: drivers, teams, manufacturers. And the evolution is natural.”

    While F1 has faced criticisms in the past for being in an endless cycle of rule changes that never seem to be given enough time to bed down, Domenicali thinks that standing still is just as bad.

    “I go back to one thing: the sport that doesn’t change is losing opportunity,” he said.

    Asked about repeated revisions of the regulations being a potential blocker of selling F1, Domenicali said: “It’s the other way around. The blocker is a sport that is not understanding the need for change.

    “I mean, you need to be attractive and understand where the mass is going.”
