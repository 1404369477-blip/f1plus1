    F1委员会将讨论针对车队的ADUO计划

    F1委员会将讨论一项针对车队的ADUO计划，旨在帮助车队摆脱成本上限的困境。

    对成本上限规则的全面改革似乎将成为周一在一级方程式委员会上讨论的关键议题，届时将辩论一项针对各队的“ADUO提案”。

    正如《赛车》杂志首次报道的那样，威廉姆斯车队老板詹姆斯·沃尔斯提出了一个支出追赶机制的构想，以帮助一级方程式车队摆脱他认为已经成为‘成本上限陷阱’的困境。

    沃尔斯认为，虽然成本上限规则在使所有车队在经济上具有可行性方面做得很好，但它也固化了既有的优势，这使得小车队难以赶上大型车队。

    他正在推动在成本上限内实施一个滑动比例追赶系统，允许排名前四名之外的车队花费更多。

    这意味着那些排名最后的车队将获得更大的财务自由，以帮助他们缩小与领先车队之间的差距。

    Vowles的提议已被添加到下周F1委员会会议的议程中，正式命名为“ADUO for teams”。

    这是指额外发展和升级机会，它存在于发动机制造商中 - 如果他们的动力单元低于基准，他们将获得额外的支出津贴、动力测试小时和认证机会。

    Vowles最初希望为该计划获得足够的支持，以便它能够在明年尽早通过F1委员会并纳入规则手册。

    为了实现这一点，需要国际汽联（FIA）、自由媒体公司（FOM）以及当前11支车队中的6支车队支持，这被称为超级多数支持。

    但来自其他五支车队的支持似乎不太可能，目前只有凯迪拉克、阿尔派恩、阿斯顿·马丁和奥迪表示支持。

    哈斯车队反对这一提议，因为它距离当前的成本上限还有一段距离，因此在支出上没有限制，而青训车队老板艾伦·帕尔曼表示他不支持这一概念。

    然而，车队的分歧意见并不意味着这个想法已经死亡，因为总共四支车队的支持就足以让这个想法在2028年得以实施。

    这将是足够早的，以便它能够帮助塑造2030年或2031年即将开始的下一个规则时代的形势。

    沃尔斯最近表示，一个折中的解决方案仍然是可能的——特别是如果国际汽联和F1支持他的计划的话——但实施可能不会迅速。

    “也许对我来说更重要的是，我有国际汽联和一级方程式的支持，”他说。

    “我认为我们还没有一个足够准确的指导方向，我们需要采取的是正确的，对这项运动有益的方向，这仍然在幕后讨论中。”

    “我能说的是，支持是存在的。”

    ““我认为这不会一夜之间发生。正如我之前提到的，我想要确保的是，我们在下一次规则变更之前把它做好，这个时间点还比较远，所以我们有足够的时间。”"

    ““但我的怀疑是，在接下来的几个月里，你将看到越来越多的讨论。”"

    ““但Vowles的想法并不是唯一受到关注的成本上限变化，因为这些讨论似乎将引发关于更广泛改革的争论。”"

    F1的顶级车队从“ADUO车队”这一想法中获益甚少，因为最大的受益者将是那些希望关闭这些车队的车队。

    但这并不意味着大型车队对当前成本上限规则的工作方式感到满意，因为他们也在寻求对规则的改变——以帮助F1保持竞争力。

    其中最热门的话题之一是当前成本上限如何影响员工保留以及F1整体保持最优秀工程师的能力。

    在成本上限下运营的车队被迫控制薪资，这导致许多F1车队在与那些被科技公司高薪吸引的才华横溢的人员争夺中处于劣势。

    也有许多关于员工在其他赛车系列（除了F1）中能够赚取更多收入的例子，因为成本上限已经对工资设定了上限。

    在全球生活成本危机的背景下，这个问题的重要性日益增加，因为F1的成本上限与通货膨胀紧密相连，所以车队无法提高工资以满足员工的需求。

    现在正在逐渐形成的一个强有力的论点是，成本上限应该演变并区分零部件支出和人员支出。

    正如F1规则长期以来所做的那样，只有组织内部薪酬最高的三名人员可以免除包含在成本上限内。

    过去曾讨论过将人数扩展到最多300人，但人们认为这会是一个过于宽泛的范围，实际上会使成本上限失效。

    在上周末的巴西大奖赛上，梅赛德斯车队老板托托·沃尔夫表示，他的车队早已提出了一个缓解机制——但这一提议被否决了。

    他对天空体育说：“我本希望得到一点提升。我们提出的想法是，随着更多青训车队人员加入车队，我们将有更多的资金来支付他们更好的薪水，而不是在最初的五到七年内压低薪水。

    “因此，我们向国际汽联提出了一个请求，希望给予我们一些补贴，不是更多的前翼补贴，而是希望薪水能稍微提高一些，因为成本上限压制了所有这些。但显然这并没有赢得很多好评。”

    英文原文

    Sweeping changes to cost cap rules look set to become a key area of discussion on Monday when an “ADUO for teams” proposal is debated at the Formula 1 Commission.

    As first revealed by The Race, Williams team boss James Vowles has tabled the idea of a spending catch-up mechanism to help F1 teams escape what he thinks has become a “cost cap trap”.

    Vowles argues that while the cost cap has done a great job making all teams financially viable, it has also locked in legacy advantages that make it difficult for smaller teams to catch up with the bigger squads.

    He is pushing for a sliding scale catch-up system within the cost cap that allows the teams outside of the top four to spend more.

    It would mean those teams that finish bottom of the standings get greater financial freedom to help them close the gap to squads at the front.

    Vowles’ proposal has been added to the agenda of next week’s F1 Commission meeting, with it becoming formally known as “ADUO for teams”.

    This is in reference to the ADUO (Additional Development and Upgrade Opportunities) that exists for engine manufacturers - who are given extra spending allowances, dyno hours and homologation opportunities if their power unit is adrift of the benchmark.

    Vowles had originally hoped to get enough backing for the plan for it to get voted through the F1 Commission and into the rulebook as early as next year.

    For that to happen, it would have required support from the FIA, FOM plus six of the current 11 teams for what is known as a supermajority.

    But backing from five others appears unlikely, with only Cadillac, Alpine, Aston Martin and Audi supportive so far.

    Haas is against because it is not near the current cost cap limit so is not limited on spending, while Racing Bulls team boss Alan Permane has said he does not back the concept.

    The divided opinion in the paddock does not mean that the idea is dead, though, as support from four teams in total would be enough to get it into play for 2028.

    That would be early enough for it to then help shape things for the next rules era that is due to start in 2030 or 2031.

    Vowles said recently that a compromise solution remained possible - especially with support from the FIA and F1 over his plans - but implementation may not be swift.

    “What's more important to me, perhaps, is I have the support of the FIA and Formula 1 behind it,” he said.

    “I don't think we have an accurate enough direction that we need to take it in, that is right for the sport, which is still being discussed behind the scenes.

    “What I can say is the support is there.

    “I don't think it's going to happen overnight. As I previously mentioned, what I want to make sure is that we get it right by the next regulation change, which is a little way off, so we have some time.

    “But my suspicion on it is you'll see more and more being discussed over the next few months in that regard.”

    But Vowles’ idea is not the only cost cap change that is under the spotlight, because these discussions look set to trigger arguments about wider reform.

    F1’s top teams have little to gain from the idea of an “ADUO for teams”, as the biggest beneficiaries would be those squads hoping to close them down.

    But that does not mean the big squads are satisfied with how the current cost cap rules work, as they too are seeking changes to the regulations - to help F1 stay competitive.

    One of the hottest topics among them is the way that current cost cap limits are impacting the retention of staff and the ability of F1 as a whole to keep hold of the brightest engineers.

    Teams operating under the cost cap are forced to keep a lid on salaries, and that has left many F1 teams fighting a losing battle to retain talented personnel who are getting lured away by big-money offers from tech companies.

    There are also many stories of staff being able to earn better money in other racing series outside of F1 because the cost cap has put a ceiling on wages so much.

    The issue has increased in importance amid a global cost-of-living crisis, with F1’s cost cap increase tightly linked to inflation so teams are unable to lift salaries to respond to what staff need.

    A strong argument that is now gathering some pace is for the cost cap to evolve and separate spending on parts and spending on people.

    As has long been the case with F1’s rules, only the three highest-paid personnel within an organisation are exempt from being included within the cost cap.

    There were discussions in the past about expanding this to include up to 300 staff, but it is thought that this would be too wide a net and would effectively make the cost cap ineffective.

    Speaking at the Brazilian Grand Prix last weekend, Mercedes boss Toto Wolff said that his squad had long proposed a mitigating mechanism - but this was shot down.

    He told Sky F1: “I would have loved to have a little bit of an uplift. The idea that we had was that with more junior personnel that joined the team, we would have more allowance to pay them better and not [having] the salaries suppressed in the first five, six, seven years.

    “So we've pitched that to the FIA to give us an allowance, not for more front wings but an allowance for salaries to come up a little bit because the cost cap suppresses all of that. But obviously that didn't score a lot of brownie points.”
