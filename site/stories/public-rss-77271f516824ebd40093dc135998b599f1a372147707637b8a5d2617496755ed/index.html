    2030年F1规则宣布失败背后的原因

    探讨2030年F1规则宣布失败的原因。

    未来不完美：2030年F1规则宣布失败的背后是什么？

    社交媒体上的帖子迅速被删除，暗示已经就下一代F1引擎达成协议

    国际汽联主席穆罕默德·本·苏莱姆，F1首席执行官斯蒂法诺·多梅尼卡利

    众所周知，当红衣主教团聚集选举新教皇时，成功的投票结果通过西斯廷教堂烟囱冒出的烟传递给聚集的人群：白烟表示决定，黑烟意味着又要进行一轮投票。这种做法起源于13世纪，当时红衣主教们花了三年时间来辩论克莱门特四世的继任者，激怒了当地信徒，他们把红衣主教们锁在宫殿里，只给他们面包和水维持生命。

    当周一备受期待的引擎制造商、国际汽联（FIA）和商业权益所有者之间的会议结束后，21世纪一级方程式（F1）的‘白烟’现象随之而来：国际汽联主席穆罕默德·本·苏莱姆（Mohammed Ben Sulayem）在Instagram上发布的一条帖子。

    “你们说了，我们听了。V8引擎即将到来，”他在‘资产’下面写道，这是一张带有国际汽联品牌标识的图形，展示了声波，并伴随着高转速V8引擎的清晰咆哮声。

    然后，几乎在一眨眼之间，白烟变成了黑色。Instagram帖子，以及国际汽联其他社交媒体渠道上的各种相关公告，都被删除了，只有那些有先见之明截图的人保留了这些内容。

    本·苏莱姆（Ben Sulayem）是呼吁回归比当前涡轮增压混合动力V6“更纯粹”的赛车引擎的最积极倡导者之一，他还主张提前进行这一变革。尽管在未来几个赛季中，电力部署与内燃机动力的平衡正在发生变化，但基本概念预计将保持到2031年。

    实际上，本·苏莱姆需要FIA、车队、制造商和商业权益持有者之间的共识。协调这些各方——其中一些彼此竞争——通常是一个既痛苦又折磨人的过程。

    我们必须在这个背景下看待本周的公告及其随后的记录删除。

    从各种简报中可以理解，对于一款带有较小电气元件的2.1升涡轮增压V8发动机，各方达成了广泛的一致。甚至达到这一点也要求做出巨大的妥协，因为本·苏莱姆的口头偏好是自然吸气V8发动机，带有象征性的电气输入，类似于2009年首次出现的动能回收系统（KERS）。

    主席认为简化是创造良性循环的方式：制造和开发成本会降低，可能为非制造商的发动机打开道路，而发动机本身也会更轻。这将使汽车达到更低的最低重量成为现实。

    这遭到了汽车制造商的相当大的抵制——尽管，为了进一步复杂化辩论，这些制造商并不完全一致。例如，福特和凯迪拉克，由于他们更关注美国市场，据信他们支持取消涡轮增压器，并保留一个较小但仍具重要性的电气元素。在另一端，奥迪和本田认为自然吸气是决定性的因素，并坚持混合动力化的重要元素。

    增加两个汽缸的问题要小得多，因为V8发动机在几个关键的道路汽车市场中仍然占据重要地位。在赛车方面，V6发动机由于它们的丑陋点火顺序，即使在不通过强制进气系统（这会降低声音）的情况下，发出的声音也远不如V8发动机那样令人振奋。

    虽然涡轮增压的原则很可能会保留，但它将基于较小的压缩机以较低的增压压力运行，这将减少延迟。电动机的输出将从目前的350千瓦（kW）降至一般运行的100kW，另外还有100kW可用于超车。

    虽然这是共识，但现在很清楚，周一会议中的几个与会方对实际达成的协议与监管机构有不同的看法。

    这并不一定是过程的挫折，尽管它暴露了我们接近必须设定规则的时刻时存在的一些共识不足。所有制造商似乎都同意的一点是，他们需要在年底前获得清晰度，以便有足够的发展空间来迎接2030年的推出。

    为此，国际汽联希望在12月10日于上海举行的世界汽车运动理事会会议上提出一个最终的2030年监管框架。然而，这本质上是一个盖章的过程。真正的协议必须在那时之前得到确保。

    本周的过早庆祝表明，这个协议已经足够接近，可以撰写新闻稿，但还没有到点击‘发送’的地步。也许我们应该把利益相关者锁在附近的宫殿里，只给他们面包和水，直到达成一致？

    Future imperfect: What's behind the abortive 2030 F1 rules announcement?

    Swiftly deleted social media posts suggested that an agreement had been reached on the next generation of Formula 1 engines

    FIA President Mohammed Ben Sulayem, F1 CEO Stefano Domenicali

    Famously, when the papal conclave gathers to elect a new pope, a successful vote is communicated to the huddled masses via smoke from the Sistine Chapel chimney as the chemically impregnated ballot papers are burned: white smoke signifies a decision, black means yet another round of voting. The practice derives from the 13th century, when the cardinals took three years to debate a successor to Clement IV, prompting the frustrated local faithful to lock them inside the palace with only bread and water to subsist upon.

    All this came to mind when Monday's much-anticipated meeting between the engine manufacturers, the FIA and the commercial rights holder to discuss the next generation of Formula 1 engines was followed by the 21st century F1 equivalent of white smoke billowing from the chimney: an Instagram post by FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem.

    “You spoke, we listened. V8s coming soon,” he wrote underneath the ‘asset', an FIA-branded graphic illustrating sound waves and accompanied by the unmistakeable scream of a high-revving V8 engine.

    And then, almost within the blinking of an eye, the white smoke turned black. The Instagram post, along with various related promulgations on the FIA's other social media channels, were deleted, kept for posterity only by those with the foresight to screenshot them.

    Ben Sulayem has been among the most vocal exponents not only of a return to ‘purer' racing engines than the current turbocharged hybrid V6s, but also of an earlier timing for that change. Although the balance of electrical deployment to internal combustion engine power is shifting over the next couple of seasons, the essential concept is due to remain in place until 2031.

    Realistically, Ben Sulayem requires a consensus between the FIA, the teams, the manufacturers and the commercial rights holder. Aligning these parties – some of whom are in competition with one another – is usually a process that is both tormented and tormenting.

    It's in that context we must view this week's announcements and their subsequent striking from the record.

    What is understood from various briefings is that there was broad agreement for a turbocharged 2.1-litre V8 with a smaller electrical element. Even to get to this point has required a great deal of compromise, since Ben Sulayem's spoken preference was for naturally aspirated V8s with token electrical input, similar to the Kinetic Energy Recovery System (KERS) first seen in 2009.

    The president sees simplification as the way of creating a virtuous circle: manufacturing and development would cost less, potentially opening the way for non-manufacturer engines, while the engines themselves would be lighter. This would make a lower minimum weight for the cars realistically achievable.

    This met with considerable resistance from the car manufacturers – although, to complicate the debate further, these are not all aligned with one another. Ford and Cadillac, for instance, with their broader focus on the US market, are understood to have been supportive of dropping turbos and having a smaller but still significant electrical element. At the other end of the scale, Audi and Honda saw natural aspiration as a deal-breaker and were insistent on a strong element of hybridisation.

    Adding two cylinders was much less of an issue since V8 engines still figure largely in several key road car markets. In racing terms, V6s make a much less inspiring noise owing to their ugly firing order, even when not breathing via a forced induction system which mutes the soundtrack.

    Although the principle of turbocharging will most likely remain, it will be based on smaller compressors running with lower boost pressures, which will reduce lag. The electrical motor's output will be reduced from the current maximum of 350 kilowatts (kW) to 100kW in general running, with another 100kW available to augment overtaking.

    While this was the understanding, it's clear now that several of the parties in Monday's meeting emerged with a different perception to the governing body about what had actually been agreed.

    This isn't necessarily a setback to the process, even though it has exposed a certain lack of consensus as we approach the point where the rules must be set. One thing all the manufacturers appear to agree on is that they need clarity by the end of this year to have enough development runway for a 2030 introduction.

    To that end, the FIA wants to present a definitive 2030 regulatory framework to the World Motor Sport Council meeting in Shanghai on 10 December. This, though, would essentially be a rubber-stamping process. The real agreement must be secured before then.

    This week's premature celebration suggests that this agreement is close enough to have the press releases written, but not yet at the point of clicking ‘send'. Perhaps we should lock the stakeholders in a nearby palace and feed them only bread and water until unanimity breaks out?
