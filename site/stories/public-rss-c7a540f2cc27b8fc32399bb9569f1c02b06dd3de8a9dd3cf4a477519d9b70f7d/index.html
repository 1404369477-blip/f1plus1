    塞恩斯解释他关于F1 2030年的引人关注的DRS提议

    塞恩斯和维斯塔潘透露了他们对F1 2030年下一套规则的期望清单

    随着F1接近敲定其2030年回归V8动力单元的总体原则，一些领先车手已详细阐述如何让这些规则成为有吸引力的产品。

    F1计划在2030年改用全新的涡轮V8发动机，搭配最高200kW的较小电动单元和可持续燃料。这应使发动机更简单、更便宜、更轻，从而相比如今常受批评的动力单元减少对电池能量的依赖。

    通过在12月12日于上海举行的下一次国际汽联世界汽车运动理事会会议上正式批准V8动力单元的方案，F1和国际汽联为自己争取了时间，以便妥善完善底盘和空气动力学规则，这些规则应与V8动力单元协同工作，从而呈现更好的赛道表现。

    上周末的拉斯维加斯大奖赛让这项赛事对下一个规则周期真正想要什么有了更多思考。在拉斯维加斯街道赛道上，F1奉献了一场引人入胜的视觉盛宴，创纪录地出现了35次领先变化，这得益于强大的尾流效应和动力单元的增压模式相结合，导致了所谓的拉锯式比赛，领先赛车在直道上往往成为活靶子。

    尽管有人争论这是否是F1希望更多看到的场面，还是拉斯维加斯过于人为化而适得其反，但这进一步凸显了开发动力单元参数与空气动力学超车辅助手段相结合的重要性，这些手段可以逐站微调，以呈现扎实的观赛效果，同时又不让超车变得过于轻而易举。

    这促使威廉姆斯车手、大奖赛车手协会理事卡洛斯·塞恩斯提出了一个关于如何在2030年调整类似DRS的超车系统的有趣想法，他建议在直道上提供增压，帮助追赶的车手在出弯后立即受益。这将抵消因跟随前车穿过其乱流而损失的过弯速度。

    塞恩斯解释道：“我认为对于2030年，我有一个关于我希望它变成什么样的想法。我觉得速度差应该更多地出现在直道开头，当你试图获得尾流的时候，而不是在直道末端，那时超车和速度差都太高了。这也会让更多超车取决于车手的技巧，而不是简单地超过前面的车。”

    “超车模式、一键加速、DRS，随便你怎么称呼它，应该在出弯时启动，这样你就有一个额外的动力提升，比如说，来弥补你在弯道中[由于脏空气]损失的时间，但这个提升不应该帮助你完成超车。我认为这个提升应该帮助你重新进入非常接近的尾流，然后利用气流和剩余的刹车点来做出你自己的动作。”

    与其在赛车已经接近极限速度时启动直线加速，这也会避免造成危险的最高速度差，塞恩斯说这实际上让车手不敢防守自己的位置。

    塞恩斯补充道：“DRS也带来了一些奇怪的超车，当你的最高速度差达到30公里/小时，你来得太快了，即使你在前面，你也太害怕去防守和挡住他，因为速度差太大了。所以这就是我的想法，我希望它变成这样并以此比赛。类似于DRS和超车模式，当你进入一定距离时你就获得那个优势，因为你是通过速度、通过不得不承受脏空气并设法让自己进入某个特定机会来赢得这个优势的。”

    目前的设想是，F1 下一代动力单元的 KERS 单元在标准模式下可提供 100kW 电能，另有 100kW 的加速模式，其启用方式尚未完全确定，总能量回收为 200kW。

    下一代引擎将不再需要主动空气动力学，但国际汽联并未排除将这种一键加速系统与尾翼上回归的 DRS 超车装置搭配使用。

    尽管拉斯维加斯站展示了今年直线模式下尾流效应可以多么关键，但四届世界冠军马克斯·维斯塔潘想知道，能否让赛车阻力足够大，从而不再需要 DRS。

    “你仍然可以设计出一套规则，让赛车在直线上阻力超大，这样你就不必那么依赖 DRS，”维斯塔潘建议道。“对我来说，一键加速系统没问题，但它需要是一台容易得多的引擎。比如每次我做表演圈，开 小红牛7 之类的车，开起来就是很舒服，因为油门响应好，一切都很轻松，车开起来很顺，赛车不抖，没有迟滞之类的。就是比我们现在的车好开得多。”

    “我知道它需要某种混合动力元素，可能是涡轮增压器，因为否则在某些赛道上你甚至不被允许驾驶，因为声音太吵了。这没问题，只要它不凌驾于自然的驾驶元素之上，而目前这种情况正在发生。”

    Carlos Sainz explains his intriguing DRS proposal for F1 2030

    Carlos Sainz and Max Verstappen have revealed what's on their wishlist for Formula 1's next regulations in 2030

    With F1 close to signing off on the overarching tenets of its 2030 return to V8 power units, some of its leading drivers have fleshed out ideas of how to make those into a compelling product.

    F1 is set to move to all-new turbo V8 engines in 2030, with a smaller electric unit of up to 200kW and sustainable fuel. That should make engines simpler, cheaper and lighter, and therefore reduce the reliance on energy from the battery compared to today's oft-criticised formula.

    By signing off the V8 move at the next FIA World Motor Sport Council gathering in Shanghai on 12 December, F1 and the FIA are giving themselves time to properly flesh out the chassis and aerodynamic regulations that should work in unison with the V8 power units to produce a better on-track show.

    Last weekend's Las Vegas Grand Prix has given the series some food for though about what it really wants from its next rules cycle. At the Las Vegas street circuit, F1 delivered an absorbing spectacle featuring a record 35 lead changes, aided by a combination of the powerful slipstreaming effect and the boost mode from the power unit, which led to so-called yo-yo racing as the lead car often ended up a sitting duck on the straight.

    While there was debate on whether that's the kind of show F1 wants to see more of, or whether Vegas was too artificial for its own good, it further highlighted the importance of developing a combination of power unit parameters and aerodynamics overtaking aids that can be fine-tuned on a race-to-race basis to deliver a solid spectacle without making overtaking too trivial either.

    It prompted Williams driver and Grand Prix Drivers' Association director Carlos Sainz to come up with an intriguing idea on how to tune a DRS-like overtaking system for 2030, suggesting a straightline boost that aids chasing drivers coming right out of the corners. That would offset the loss of cornering speed from following a car in its dirty air.

    "I think for 2030 I have an idea of where I would like it to be," Sainz explained. "I think the delta on speed should come more at the beginning of the straight when you are trying to get the slipstream rather than at the end of the straight when the overtaking and the delta speed is too high. It would also leave more overtaking down to driver skill rather than simply sailing past the car in front.

    "The power on the overtake mode, push to pass, DRS, whatever you want to call it, should happen exiting the corner, so that you have an extra power boost, let's say, to catch up what you are losing in the corner [due to dirty air] but the boost shouldn't then help you to finish the overtake. I think the boost should help you manage to get back into the very close slipstream and then use the draft and the rest of the braking point to make your own move."

    Rather than a straightline boost that kicks in when cars are already approaching terminal velocity, it would also avoid creating a dangerous top speed deficit, which Sainz said is actually discouraging drivers from defending their position.

    "The weird overtaking that happened also a bit with DRS is when you get delta top speeds of 30 km/h and you arrive so quick that even when you are in front you are too scared to defend and cut him off, because the delta speed is so high," Sainz added. "So that is my idea of how I would like it to be and go racing with. Similar to a DRS and overtake mode that when you get into a certain distance you get that advantage, because you earn it through speed and through having to suffer the dirty air and manage to get yourself into a certain opportunity to get that advantage."

    The current thinking is for the KERS unit of F1's next power units to make 100kW of electric energy available in standard mode, with an additional 100kW of boost mode accessed in a way that has yet to be fully defined and a total harvest of 200kW.

    The next generation of engines will no longer require active aerodynamics, but the FIA hasn't ruled out pairing that push-to-pash system with a return of the DRS overtaking device on the rear wing.

    While Las Vegas showed how crucial the role of slipstreaming can be with this year's Straight Mode, four-time world champion Max Verstappen is wondering if cars can be made draggy enough so that DRS is no longer necessary.

    "You can still come up with a formula where maybe a car is just super draggy in a straight line, so you don't need to rely so much on DRS," Verstappen suggested. "A push-to-pass system for me is fine, but it needs to be a much easier engine. Every time I do a show run, for example in an RB7 or whatever, it's just so nice to drive because the throttle responds, everything is just easy, it drives off nice, the car is not shaking about, it has no lag, stuff like that. It's just a lot nicer to drive than what we have currently.

    "I know it needs to be some kind of hybrid element, probably turbocharger, because otherwise you are not even allowed to drive at some tracks anymore because it's too loud. That is fine, as long as it's not overruling the natural driving element, which of course at the moment is happening."
